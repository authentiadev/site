Why AI adoption needs more than training

Plan the leadership, workflow, communication and learning changes that help employees put AI to work with confidence.

Change Management · 5 min read · By Authentia · Updated October 29, 2026

AI adoption needs a clear business purpose, visible sponsorship, practical changes to roles and workflows, and time to learn. Assess readiness, explain what will change, equip managers, agree review and escalation responsibilities, and measure performance in everyday work. Use those findings to adjust the rollout with the people who own it.

AI adoption asks people to change familiar work. They need to understand the purpose, practise new tasks and know where their judgement remains essential. Training supports that process. Leadership decisions, workload, information and everyday management also shape whether the new approach becomes useful.

For a mid-sized or large organisation, a practical change plan connects those parts around a specific initiative. It gives the people responsible for rollout a shared view of what must happen, who needs support and how progress will be assessed.

Start with readiness for a particular change

Describe the workflow and the improvement being sought. Then speak with the people who do the work, manage it and receive its output. Ask where the proposed approach fits, which information is unreliable and what would make it difficult to use.

Check capacity as well as enthusiasm. A team managing a busy period or another system change may need a different sequence. Record dependencies, concerns and unresolved decisions in a short readiness brief, with owners for the issues that could delay the next step.

Give the sponsor decisions to make

A sponsor should be able to explain why the change matters, which outcome takes priority and what support the team will receive. Agree which decisions require their involvement: allocating time to learn, resolving competing priorities or accepting a narrower first rollout.

Microsoft's 2026 Work Trend Index connects reported AI impact with organisational conditions including leadership, manager support and working practices. Its survey covers people already using AI across ten countries. It provides useful context for treating adoption as a management responsibility; it does not establish that a particular change programme causes better results.

Make changes to roles and workflows explicit

Map what employees will do differently from the start of a task through to its completed output. Identify who supplies information, checks the result, handles exceptions and authorises any consequential action. Include the route people will use when the system is unavailable.

Managers need enough detail to answer practical questions. Can someone correct a draft directly? Which cases need specialist review? Who maintains the source information? Workflow and operating model design helps turn those questions into an arrangement people can follow.

Explain the change through the work

Communication should describe what is changing, when it affects each group and where people can raise concerns. Use a realistic task to show the expected process, including its limits. Be clear about decisions still under discussion. Give managers a short briefing and a consistent route for questions they cannot answer. Feedback from employees can reveal process problems that the project team has missed.

Plan learning people can use

Build learning around the tasks each role will perform. Give users approved examples, time to practise and a clear explanation of required checks. Include guidance on information handling and escalation, agreed with the relevant business, privacy and security owners. These responsibilities also belong in a practical AI governance plan.

Skills England's 2026 AI upskilling research briefing, drawing on workshops, employer research and case studies, recommends learning linked to actual tasks, flexible formats and protected time. Its guidance applies to England; the principles offer useful questions for planning learning elsewhere.

Decide who will answer questions after the first session and how guidance will be updated. A manager check-in, shared examples and access to an experienced colleague can help employees apply what they have learned.

Measure the working change

Choose a small set of measures before rollout. Include whether people can complete the intended workflow, follow the required checks and resolve exceptions. Add the business outcomes the initiative is meant to improve, such as completeness, service turnaround or avoidable rework.

The ONS analysis of AI in UK businesses cautions that a headline adoption measure can treat light use and embedded, production-level adoption alike. That limitation matters in a change plan: attendance and logins tell you something about participation, while observation and workflow results help establish what has changed in practice.

Use checkpoints to guide the next wave

Review feedback and performance with the business owner before expanding. The next step might involve clearer guidance, a revised handoff, additional practice or a smaller scope. Agree who will make the decision and which conditions must be met. Revisit the plan when the tool, process or intended users change.
